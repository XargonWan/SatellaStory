# The Satellaview System

## Overview

The **Satellaview** (サテラビュー) was a satellite modem peripheral for the Super Famicom, released by Nintendo in 1995 exclusively in Japan. It connected to the console via the expansion port and received data broadcasts via satellite through a partnership with the radio station **St.GIGA**.[^1]

The system allowed users to download games, magazines, and additional content over satellite during designated broadcast windows. It also featured **SoundLink** games, which synchronized gameplay with live radio broadcasts including voice narration and music.[^2]

## Technical Specifications

| Component | Details |
|-----------|---------|
| Release Date | April 23, 1995 (BS-X cartridge launch)[^3] |
| Price | ~¥14,000 (~$150 USD)[^4] |
| Broadcast Partner | St.GIGA (BS analog channel 5)[^5] |
| Broadcast Hours | 4:00 PM - 7:00 PM daily[^6] |
| Required Accessories | BS-X cartridge, Memory Pack (1 megabit flash RAM), satellite antenna subscription |

The Satellaview connected to the Super Famicom via its **EXT port** (the expansion port on the bottom of the console). It required a satellite dish tuned to the BS-5 channel (NHK's satellite channel) and a subscription to St.GIGA's data broadcasting service.[^7]

## The BS-X Cartridge

The **BS-X: Sore wa Namae o Nusumareta Machi no Monogatari** (BS-X: The Story of the Town Whose Name Was Stolen) cartridge served as the operating system and user interface for the Satellaview. It was required to boot the system and provided:

- A graphical town interface where the player could navigate
- A download manager for receiving broadcasts
- A memory pack manager for saving content
- An event plaza for time-limited downloadable content[^8]

The cartridge contained the entirety of the Satellaview's browser/OS software. Without it, the Satellaview hardware was non-functional.[^9]

## The Memory Pack

The **Memory Pack** was a 1-megabit (128KB) flash RAM cartridge that plugged into the BS-X cartridge (similar in form factor to a Super Game Boy). It stored downloaded games, save data, and configuration settings. The pack used a battery-backed SRAM + flash memory combination and functioned similarly to later Nintendo Power flash carts.[^10]

## Broadcast Types

### Downloadable Games
Full games or add-on data that could be downloaded and played at any time after broadcast. These were stored on the Memory Pack.[^11]

### SoundLink Games
Real-time interactive games that synchronized with live radio broadcasts. These games featured voice acting, commentary, and music that changed week-to-week. SoundLink games could not be saved -- they existed only during the broadcast window.[^12]

### Magazines and News
Downloadable "kabe shinbun" (wall newspaper) content and game magazines that provided news, tips, and game-related articles.[^13]

## Service Timeline

| Date | Event |
|------|-------|
| April 23, 1995 | BS-X cartridge and Satellaview service launch[^14] |
| 1995-1996 | Initial SoundLink broadcasts |
| 1997 | Peak of service; major franchises (Zelda, Fire Emblem, Mario) featured |
| 1999 | Service begins winding down |
| June 1999 | Last regular Satellaview broadcasts[^15] |
| Early 2000 | St.GIGA dissolves; Satellaview service officially ends[^16] |
| March 31, 2005 | End of BS analog broadcasting (St.GIGA/WINJ)[^17] |

## Business Model Failure

The Satellaview's business model was complex: users paid for the hardware, a monthly subscription to St.GIGA, and then the games themselves were "free" during broadcasts. However, Nintendo struggled to attract advertisers and sponsors for broadcasts. The specialized nature of the content and Japan's economic downturn in the late 1990s contributed to the system's commercial failure.[^18] Nintendo eventually withdrew from the partnership, leaving St.GIGA to continue solo broadcasts until the service's end.[^19]

## Legacy

Despite its commercial failure, the Satellaview has become a subject of intense interest among video game preservationists and historians. Many Satellaview-exclusive games were never released through any other medium and today survive only through ROM dumps, VHS recordings of broadcasts, and fan restoration projects.[^20]
